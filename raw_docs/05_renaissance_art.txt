The Renaissance marked a profound shift in European art, moving away from the flat, symbolic style of the medieval period toward a renewed interest in realism and human anatomy. Artists began studying classical Greek and Roman sculpture as models for proportion and form. This revival of classical ideals gave the period its name, drawn from the French word for "rebirth."

Linear perspective was one of the most transformative techniques developed during this era, allowing painters to create a convincing illusion of depth on a flat surface. Filippo Brunelleschi is often credited with formalizing the mathematical principles behind perspective in early fifteenth-century Florence. Painters like Masaccio quickly applied these principles, producing works with a sense of three-dimensional space that had rarely been achieved before.

Patronage played a central role in shaping Renaissance art, with wealthy families and the Catholic Church commissioning most major works. The Medici family of Florence famously supported artists like Botticelli and Michelangelo, using art as a display of both piety and political power. This system of patronage meant that artistic subject matter was often closely tied to the interests and values of powerful sponsors.

Leonardo da Vinci exemplified the era's fascination with combining art and science, filling notebooks with anatomical studies, engineering sketches, and botanical observations. His painting technique of sfumato, a subtle blending of tones without harsh outlines, gave works like the Mona Lisa their famously soft, lifelike quality. Da Vinci's approach reflected a broader Renaissance belief that careful observation of nature was essential to great art.

The influence of the Renaissance extended far beyond Italy, spreading to Northern Europe where artists like Jan van Eyck developed their own distinct innovations, particularly in oil painting technique. Northern Renaissance art tended to emphasize minute detail and texture, contrasting with the idealized forms favored in Italy. Together, these regional movements reshaped the trajectory of Western art for centuries to come.
