A blockchain is fundamentally a distributed ledger, a record of transactions maintained across many independent computers rather than a single central authority. Each new set of transactions is grouped into a block, which is cryptographically linked to the previous block, forming an unbroken chain. This structure makes it extremely difficult to alter historical records without detection, since doing so would require changing every subsequent block as well.

Consensus mechanisms are what allow a blockchain network to agree on the validity of new transactions without relying on a trusted central party. Proof of work, used by Bitcoin, requires participants to solve computationally intensive puzzles in order to add a new block to the chain. This process, often called mining, secures the network but consumes significant amounts of energy, prompting the development of alternative mechanisms.

Proof of stake has emerged as a popular alternative to proof of work, selecting validators based on the amount of cryptocurrency they are willing to lock up as collateral. This approach dramatically reduces energy consumption compared to mining-based systems, since it eliminates the need for intensive computational puzzles. Ethereum's transition from proof of work to proof of stake in 2022 was one of the most significant events in the history of blockchain technology.

Smart contracts extend blockchain technology beyond simple currency transactions, allowing self-executing code to run automatically when predetermined conditions are met. Developers can use smart contracts to build decentralized applications ranging from financial services to digital collectibles without relying on traditional intermediaries. This programmability has been a major driver of blockchain adoption beyond its original use case as a currency system.

Despite considerable enthusiasm, blockchain technology continues to face significant challenges related to scalability, energy use, and regulatory uncertainty. Many networks struggle to process transactions as quickly as traditional centralized payment systems, particularly during periods of high demand. Ongoing research into layer-two scaling solutions and alternative consensus models aims to address these limitations while preserving the core benefits of decentralization.
