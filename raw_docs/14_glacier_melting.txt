Glaciers form over centuries as accumulated snow compresses into dense ice, and their slow but persistent flow has shaped landscapes across the planet. Today, the vast majority of the world's glaciers are retreating at an accelerating pace, driven primarily by rising global temperatures. Scientists use satellite imagery and ground measurements to track these changes with increasing precision over time.

The consequences of glacier melt extend far beyond the mountains and polar regions where the ice itself is located. Meltwater from glaciers contributes significantly to global sea level rise, threatening coastal communities and low-lying island nations around the world. Even relatively small increases in sea level can dramatically increase the frequency and severity of coastal flooding during storms.

Many communities around the world depend on glacial meltwater as a critical source of fresh water, particularly during dry seasons when other sources run low. Rivers fed by glaciers in the Himalayas, for example, support drinking water and agriculture for hundreds of millions of people across South Asia. As glaciers shrink, the reliability of this water supply becomes increasingly uncertain, raising concerns about future water security in the region.

Glacial retreat also has significant effects on local ecosystems, altering habitats that many species have adapted to over thousands of years. Some cold-adapted species face shrinking suitable habitat as ice retreats to higher elevations, while newly exposed land creates opportunities for other species to colonize. These shifting ecological dynamics can take decades or longer to stabilize into a new equilibrium.

Efforts to slow glacier melt are closely tied to broader climate change mitigation, since reducing greenhouse gas emissions remains the most effective long-term solution. Some researchers have proposed more localized interventions, such as covering sections of glaciers with reflective fabric to reduce melting during summer months. While such measures may offer temporary relief in specific locations, most scientists agree they cannot substitute for addressing the root causes of global warming.
