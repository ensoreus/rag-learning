Space debris refers to the growing collection of defunct satellites, spent rocket stages, and fragments left over from decades of human activity in orbit. Estimates suggest there are hundreds of millions of debris pieces currently circling the Earth, ranging in size from paint flecks to entire dead satellites. Even small fragments pose a serious threat due to the extremely high velocities involved in orbital collisions.

The Kessler syndrome describes a theoretical scenario in which collisions between debris objects generate additional fragments, triggering a cascading chain reaction that increases debris density over time. This process could eventually render certain orbital regions unusable for future spacecraft due to the sheer volume of hazardous debris. While a full Kessler syndrome event has not yet occurred, several near-miss collisions have highlighted the growing seriousness of the risk.

Tracking space debris has become an essential function for space agencies and increasingly for private companies operating large satellite constellations. Ground-based radar and telescope networks monitor thousands of objects larger than ten centimeters, issuing collision warnings when objects approach dangerously close to active satellites. Smaller debris, while still dangerous, often falls below the resolution of current tracking systems, making it far harder to avoid.

The rapid growth of satellite mega-constellations, designed to provide global internet coverage, has raised new concerns about the future sustainability of low Earth orbit. Companies launching thousands of satellites must now account for end-of-life disposal plans to avoid contributing further to the debris problem. Regulatory bodies around the world are increasingly requiring operators to demonstrate a credible plan for deorbiting satellites at the end of their operational lives.

Various technologies have been proposed to actively remove existing debris from orbit, ranging from robotic arms and nets to harpoons and drag sails. Several demonstration missions have successfully tested these concepts in recent years, though large-scale cleanup remains prohibitively expensive at current technology levels. Most experts agree that preventing new debris through better design and disposal practices remains far more cost-effective than cleaning up existing debris.
