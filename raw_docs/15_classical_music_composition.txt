Classical music composition during the Baroque and Classical eras was governed by well-established structural conventions that shaped how composers organized their works. Sonata form, characterized by an exposition, development, and recapitulation, became a dominant structure for symphonies, concertos, and chamber music. Composers like Haydn and Mozart used this framework extensively, exploring its possibilities across hundreds of compositions.

Harmony in classical composition relies on a system of chords and key relationships that create a sense of tension and resolution throughout a piece. Composers deliberately move away from a home key to build musical tension, then return to resolve that tension and provide a satisfying sense of closure. This interplay between tension and resolution forms the emotional backbone of much Western classical music, even in pieces without any lyrics.

Orchestration, the art of assigning musical ideas to specific instruments, became increasingly sophisticated as composers gained a deeper understanding of each instrument's unique capabilities. Beethoven expanded the size and role of the orchestra significantly, giving greater prominence to winds and brass than earlier Classical-era composers typically did. His use of orchestration helped pave the way for the large, expressive orchestras that would define the Romantic era that followed.

Counterpoint, the technique of combining multiple independent melodic lines, reached extraordinary heights of complexity in the works of Johann Sebastian Bach. His fugues demonstrate a mastery of weaving several melodies together so that each remains distinct while contributing to a unified whole. This contrapuntal tradition, while less central in later periods, continued to influence composers who studied Bach's technical innovations for generations afterward.

Modern composers have continued to build on and challenge these traditional structures, experimenting with atonality, extended techniques, and entirely new approaches to organizing sound. Composers like Arnold Schoenberg rejected traditional tonal harmony altogether, developing the twelve-tone technique as an alternative organizing principle. This ongoing evolution demonstrates that classical composition remains a living tradition rather than a fixed set of historical rules.
