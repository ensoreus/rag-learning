Sourdough bread relies on a natural fermentation process rather than commercial yeast, using a living culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria. This culture, commonly called a starter, must be fed regularly with flour and water to stay active. Bakers often maintain their starters for years, sometimes passing them down through generations as a kind of living heirloom.

The fermentation process gives sourdough its distinctive tangy flavor, which develops from the lactic and acetic acids produced by the bacteria. Longer fermentation times generally produce a more pronounced sourness, while shorter proofs yield a milder taste. Temperature plays a crucial role here, since warmer environments accelerate fermentation and cooler ones slow it down considerably.

Achieving the right texture in sourdough requires careful attention to hydration, the ratio of water to flour in the dough. Higher hydration doughs tend to produce a more open, irregular crumb with larger air pockets, prized by many artisan bakers. However, wetter doughs are also stickier and more difficult to shape, demanding practice and a gentle touch.

Unlike commercial yeast breads, sourdough benefits from a long, slow proof, often lasting many hours or even overnight in the refrigerator. This extended fermentation not only develops flavor but also breaks down gluten and starches, making the bread easier to digest for some people. Many bakers find that a cold retard in the fridge also makes the dough easier to score and shape before baking.

Baking sourdough typically requires high heat and steam, which helps the crust develop its characteristic crackling texture. A Dutch oven is a popular tool for home bakers because it traps steam released from the dough during the first phase of baking. The result, when done well, is a loaf with a crisp, blistered crust and a chewy, flavorful interior.
