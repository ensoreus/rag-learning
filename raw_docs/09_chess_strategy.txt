Chess strategy involves long-term planning based on the structural features of a position, distinct from tactics, which focus on short-term calculated sequences. A player thinking strategically considers factors like pawn structure, piece activity, and control of key squares. Strong players often describe strategy as answering the question of what the position calls for, rather than simply what move seems clever.

Control of the center has long been considered a foundational principle of chess strategy, since central squares grant pieces greater mobility and influence over the board. Classical chess theory, associated with players like Wilhelm Steinitz, emphasized direct occupation of the center with pawns. Later, hypermodern theorists like Aron Nimzowitsch argued that controlling the center from a distance with pieces could be just as effective.

Pawn structure often dictates the strategic character of a game, creating long-lasting weaknesses or strengths that persist even after most pieces have been traded. Doubled pawns, isolated pawns, and passed pawns each carry distinct strategic implications that skilled players learn to exploit or defend against. Because pawns cannot move backward, decisions about pawn structure tend to have consequences that last for the remainder of the game.

The concept of piece activity refers to how effectively a piece can influence the board, regardless of its nominal material value. A knight trapped in a corner may be worth far less in practice than its textbook value suggests, while a well-placed bishop can dominate an entire diagonal. Strong players frequently sacrifice small amounts of material to improve the activity of their remaining pieces.

Endgame strategy differs significantly from the middlegame, as reduced material means that king safety becomes less critical and king activity becomes an asset. In many endgames, the king transforms from a piece to be protected into an active fighting piece that supports pawn advances. Mastering endgame technique, including key concepts like opposition and zugzwang, is often what separates strong players from merely good ones.
