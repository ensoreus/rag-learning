Coffee cultivation begins with two dominant species grown commercially worldwide, Coffea arabica and Coffea robusta, each suited to different growing conditions. Arabica plants generally require higher altitudes and more stable temperatures, producing beans widely regarded as having a smoother, more complex flavor profile. Robusta, by contrast, tolerates harsher growing conditions and produces beans with higher caffeine content, often used in espresso blends for its bold, bitter character.

The concept of terroir, borrowed from winemaking, plays a significant role in shaping the final flavor of coffee, influenced by soil composition, altitude, and local climate. Coffee grown at higher elevations tends to develop more slowly, allowing sugars and acids to accumulate in ways that produce more complex, nuanced flavors. This is part of why single-origin coffees from specific regions, such as Ethiopia's Yirgacheffe, are prized for their distinctive and recognizable taste characteristics.

Processing methods after harvest have a dramatic impact on the final cup, with washed, natural, and honey processing each producing noticeably different flavor outcomes. Washed processing removes the fruit pulp before drying, generally producing a cleaner, brighter cup with pronounced acidity. Natural processing, in which the coffee cherry dries intact around the bean, tends to produce a fruitier, heavier-bodied cup due to sugars absorbed from the fruit during drying.

Coffee farming supports the livelihoods of an estimated twenty-five million farmers worldwide, the vast majority of whom operate small family farms rather than large industrial plantations. Fluctuating global coffee prices can have an outsized impact on these smallholder farmers, who often have limited ability to absorb sudden market downturns. Fair trade and direct trade certification programs have emerged partly in response to these vulnerabilities, aiming to secure more stable and equitable prices for farmers.

Climate change poses a growing threat to coffee cultivation, as rising temperatures shrink the geographic regions suitable for growing high-quality arabica beans. Researchers estimate that suitable coffee-growing land could decline significantly by mid-century under current warming trends, threatening both livelihoods and global supply. This has spurred increased interest in breeding more heat-tolerant coffee varieties and exploring wild coffee species as potential sources of genetic resilience.
